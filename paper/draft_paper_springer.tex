\title[Distance Decay in Zero-Shot Calibration]{Distance Decay as a Primary Transferable Component: Zero-Shot Gravity Model Calibration from Trip-Length Distributions}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Thinh} \sur{Nguyen}}\email{nqt900@gmail.com}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Aarista Technologies}, \orgaddress{\city{Ho Chi Minh City}, \country{Vietnam}}}

\abstract{Predicting commuting flows in cities without access to individual travel trajectories is a central challenge in urban mobility modeling: most high-accuracy methods require local origin-destination (OD) surveys or individual-level records for calibration, limiting their applicability in data-scarce or privacy-regulated environments. This study investigates whether aggregate trip-length distributions---population-level histograms of traveled distances available from mobile network operators and tech platforms---contain sufficient information to recover the city-specific distance-decay structure needed for OD-free gravity model calibration. We distinguish two transfer settings: an \emph{OD-free calibration} setting that uses only target-city aggregate trip-length distributions (but not individual OD records), and a \emph{pure zero-shot fallback} that uses no target mobility data whatsoever. Evaluating across 50 US metropolitan areas (25 source cities for training and 25 held-out target cities for evaluation), we show that decay parameters estimated from aggregate histograms alone correlate strongly with those fitted from complete OD matrices, with a negligible downstream prediction gap under oracle outflow conditions. Through factorial ablation and game-theoretic Shapley value analysis, we show that distance decay dominates predictive capacity within the investigated gravity framework, indicating it functions as the primary transferable component of spatial interaction. Under oracle outflow conditions, aggregate-recovered decay achieves downstream performance statistically equivalent to OD-fitted decay. In the fully survey-free 25-city evaluation using independently sourced Meta MDM decay, the proposed model remains below DeepGravity but provides a highly interpretable and data-efficient OD-free alternative.}

\keywords{Urban mobility, distance decay calibration, gravity models, aggregate mobility data, zero-shot transfer, open-data modeling}

\section{Introduction}

Predicting origin-destination (OD) travel flows is essential for urban transport planning and infrastructure design \citep{barbosa2018human,gonzalez2008understanding,song2010limits}. However, traditional travel surveys and proprietary cellular records are highly restricted due to cost, commercial licensing, and privacy concerns. Consequently, a central challenge in urban mobility modeling is the zero-shot cross-city transfer problem: predicting commuting flows in a target city where no local OD surveys are available.

While recent deep learning approaches achieve high accuracy, they require abundant individual-level OD records for training, limiting their generalizability and raising privacy concerns. In contrast, classical gravity models \citep{wilson1971} offer physical interpretability and structural simplicity but require local calibration of their distance-decay functions. This raises a fundamental scientific question: \textbf{Can aggregate mobility distributions, which do not reveal individual OD pairs, serve as a reliable calibration signal for recovering city-specific distance-decay functions?}

In this paper, we address this question by proposing a three-tier conceptual framework: (1) we identify the \emph{transferable quantity} of spatial interaction as the city-specific distance-decay function, (2) we utilize the target-city aggregate trip-length distribution (TLD) as an \emph{observable projection} that allows this transferable quantity to be identified without revealing individual OD pairs, and (3) we apply this recovered decay in a \emph{zero-shot calibration} setting to predict commuting flows. Under the primary \textbf{OD-free calibration setting}, the model recovers distance-decay parameters from target-city aggregate TLDs (e.g., Meta Movement Distribution Maps). In the \textbf{pure zero-shot fallback}, the model uses a national decay prior derived from source cities. We show that aggregate trip-length histograms contain sufficient information to recover the city-specific distance-decay structure with high fidelity, and our game-theoretic Shapley analysis attributes the vast majority of predictive capacity to distance decay within the investigated framework---consistent with the interpretation that distance decay functions as the primary transferable component of spatial interaction.

To investigate these relationships systematically, we organize the investigation around three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{(Decay Recovery)} Can aggregate trip-length distributions recover the city-specific distance-decay structure without access to individual OD trajectories?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{(Decay Impact)} How does distance decay impact zero-shot gravity transfer of commuting flows, and to what extent does its recovery from trip-length distributions drive transfer performance?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{(Morphological Scope)} Under what urban morphological conditions does aggregate-calibrated decay perform most effectively, and what physical geography factors govern those conditions?
\end{enumerate}

Our empirical investigation across 50 US metropolitan areas provides three specific contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Decay Recovery from Aggregate TLD (RQ1)}: We demonstrate that aggregate trip-length distributions can successfully recover the dominant shape of city-specific distance decay, especially the power-law exponent $\gamma$ ($r = 0.909$), achieving a near-identical downstream CPC ($0.11\%$ average gap) when target production totals are controlled using oracle outflows.
  \item \textbf{Component-Level Shapley Analysis (RQ2)}: Our game-theoretic Shapley analysis shows that distance decay dominates predictive capacity gain (accounting for 85.8\% of the total gain) specifically within this decomposed gravity game, rather than representing a universal claim across all urban mobility model structures.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory Morphology Analysis (RQ3)}: We provide exploratory analysis suggesting that physics-constrained gravity models perform relatively better in physically constrained coastal cities (narrowing the gap to DeepGravity to 5.8\%), though we treat these findings as preliminary due to the small morphological group sizes.
\end{enumerate}

These findings suggest that distance decay represents a structurally transferable object across metropolitan areas, and that projecting spatial interactions onto the one-dimensional trip-length distribution provides a privacy-preserving measurement sufficient to identify this object. The resulting model achieves reasonable performance across most urban morphologies, though a performance gap versus high-capacity neural baselines remains (5.8\%--10.3\% depending on city type). A pure zero-shot fallback using only a national decay prior retains 92.4\% of oracle performance, providing an additional deployment pathway for severely data-scarce settings. The rest of the paper provides systematic empirical verification organized around these three research questions.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Classical and Structured Mobility Models}
Gravity models \citep{wilson1971,tanner1961} and radiation models \citep{Simini2012universal,alis2021generalized} provide the basis of spatial interaction modeling. Gravity models employ physical metaphors to link flows to mass and distance in zones, while radiation models \citep{stouffer1940intervening} interpret the choice of destinations probabilistically on the basis of intervening opportunities. Scaling-law studies \citep{brockmann2006scaling,gonzalez2008understanding} and predictability analysis \citep{song2010limits} have shown that travel distances obey clear statistical patterns in urban settings. Both model classes offer interpretability and parsimony, but gravity models require local OD data to calibrate decay parameters, while radiation models underperform in cities where the intervening opportunities assumption breaks down due to strong directional commuting preferences or constrained network topologies.

\subsection{Deep Learning for OD Prediction}
DeepGravity \citep{simini2021} uses multi-layer perceptrons to associate urban characteristics with flow volumes. Recent advancements have included graph neural networks \citep{Rong2023goddag}, spatio-temporal networks \citep{wang2019origin}, and OSM-based commuter modeling \citep{atwal2025commuting}. These methods achieve strong predictive accuracy where training data is abundant, but require substantial target-city OD records for training and fine-tuning, limiting their applicability in data-scarce environments and raising concerns about out-of-distribution generalizability and model interpretability.

\subsection{Aggregate Mobility Data for Decay Calibration}
Today's location-aware mobile phones yield mobility signals of entire populations \citep{barbosa2018human,gonzalez2008understanding} that can be summarized to form trip-length distributions---histograms of traveled distances---that do not reveal any individual OD pairs \citep{pappalardo2023analytical}. Previous studies suggest that such distributions are indicative of the friction of urban trips and match distance-decay models \citep{brockmann2006scaling,lenormand2012universal,lenormand2016systematic}. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no existing study on using such distributions to calibrate zero-shot cross-city transfer. Importantly, while truly privacy-safe TLDs (e.g., Meta MDM) do not derive from zone-to-zone OD data, TLDs can also be synthetically aggregated from existing OD matrices for diagnostic purposes; this study evaluates both scenarios, with the independently sourced Meta MDM representing the true privacy-compliant deployment case.

\subsection{Cross-City Transfer Learning}
Transfer learning across cities is designed to forecast mobility in cities that lack OD survey data. The existing literature on cross-city transfer learning can be divided into three categories:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Similarity-based}: RegionTrans \citep{wang2018regiontrans} uses spatial similarity to align source and target regions; CrossTReS \citep{jin2022crosstres} re-weights source regions using meta-learning.
  \item \textbf{Representation learning}: Geospatial embeddings and GeoAI foundation models \citep{mai2022geospatial,mendieta2023geogpt} use representation learning to develop features for urban systems.
  \item \textbf{Domain adaptation}: STAN \citep{wang2022when} and meta-spatiotemporal networks \citep{yao2020meta} align trained models to the target domains.
\end{enumerate}
All three categories require access to target-city observations, fine-tuning, or domain-specific calibration.

\subsection{Quantifying the Relative Role of Distance Decay in Transfer}
Although distance decay is widely acknowledged as central to gravity models, its relative importance compared to other components (origin production, destination attraction) in the context of zero-shot cross-city transfer has not been systematically quantified. SHAP \citep{lundberg2017unified} offers a theoretically grounded framework for decomposing predictive contributions, but its application to cross-city component analysis in mobility models remains unexplored.

\section{Problem Setting}\label{sec:problem_setting}
To establish a rigorous evaluation framework, we define two operational deployment settings and one diagnostic experimental control, representing different operational constraints and analytical diagnostics:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Setting 1: Pure Zero-Shot Transfer}: In this setting, absolutely no target-city mobility or travel survey data (neither OD matrix nor aggregate TLD) is available. The model must rely entirely on open spatial data (OSM, WorldPop) for target features, and utilizes a fixed \emph{National Prior} for the distance-decay function derived from training cities. This represents the fallback scenario under extreme data scarcity.
  
  \item \textbf{Setting 2: OD-Free Aggregate Calibration}: This is the primary proposed operational pipeline. We assume access to target-city aggregate trip-length distributions (TLD) under the form of 1D distance-bin histograms (e.g., Meta Movement Distribution Maps). Individual zone-to-zone commuting trajectories (OD matrices) remain completely unavailable. This setting enables city-specific decay calibration while maintaining high privacy protection and zero survey requirements.
  
  \item \textbf{Oracle Diagnostic Control (Experiment A only)}: This is a controlled diagnostic used exclusively for component-level analysis and algorithm validation (Experiment A). The target city's true origin outflows ($O_i$) are used as oracle values to isolate the distance-decay estimation process from potential prediction errors in the trip production component. This is not a deployable setting; it serves to establish an upper-bound estimate of decay recovery quality, decoupled from production errors.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological Design}\label{sec:methodology}

The approach taken in this study does not attempt to learn the complete urban mobility process end-to-end. Instead, we structure our methodology around a three-tier conceptual architecture:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Transferable Object}: We hypothesize that the city-specific distance-decay function $f(d)$ represents the primary transferable component of spatial interaction, capturing the uniform spatial friction of travel that generalizes across zone pairs.
  \item \textbf{The Observable Measurement}: We utilize the aggregate trip-length distribution (TLD) as a one-dimensional projection of spatial flows. This marginal distribution acts as a privacy-preserving measurement that allows the decay function's parameters to be identified without accessing individual zone-to-zone trajectories.
  \item \textbf{The Application Setting}: We deploy the recovered decay function within a zero-shot cross-city transfer framework, combining it with GBDT-predicted production and open attraction heuristics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Decomposition Hypothesis}
We hypothesize that OD flows can be decomposed into three separable, city-independent components:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomposition}
T_{ij} = O_i \cdot \frac{A_j f(d_{ij})}{\sum_{k} A_k f(d_{ik})}
\end{equation}
where $O_i$ represents origin production capacity, $f(d_{ij})$ represents distance decay, and $A_j$ captures destination attraction. We hypothesize that: (1) each component is approximately independent, (2) the underlying mechanisms can be learned from spatial descriptors of the built environment, and (3) the learned origin production relationship transfers directly to unseen target cities, while the decay function acts as the primary transferable object recovered from target-city aggregate trip-length distributions, and attraction is computed from locally available open data. This decomposition hypothesis is directly tested by evaluating which of these three components exerts the dominant influence on predictive accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates and validates this hypothesis empirically.

\subsection{Aggregate-Based Decay Calibration}
The central methodological insight of this study is that the aggregate trip-length distribution (TLD) $p(d)$ --- a histogram of how many trips occur at each distance bin, summed across all origin-destination pairs in a city --- represents the marginal distribution of the OD matrix over distance (the decay function $f_\theta$ used here is the Tanner form defined in Section~\ref{sec:decay_function}). Formally:
\begin{equation}
p(d) = \sum_{i,j} T_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{1}[d_{ij} \approx d]
\end{equation}

In principle, the marginal $p(d)$ does not automatically represent a sufficient statistic for the decay parameters, as the number of trips observed at distance $d$ is strongly conditioned by a city's spatial opportunity distribution (the spatial arrangement of population $O_i$ and jobs $A_j$), zone geometries, and boundary effects. To address this and prevent bias from spatial configurations, we formalize the recovery of decay parameters $\theta = (\gamma, \beta)$ using an exposure-corrected likelihood model.

Let $y_b$ be the observed commuter count in distance bin $b \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, obtained from the target-city aggregate trip-length histogram. Under the production-constrained gravity framework, the predicted flow between zones $i$ and $j$ under decay parameters $\theta$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:predicted_flow_mle}
\hat{T}_{ij}(\theta) = O_i \frac{A_j f_\theta(d_{ij})}{\sum_{k} A_k f_\theta(d_{ik})}
\end{equation}
where $O_i$ is the target outflow (oracle in Experiment A; GBDT-estimated in Experiments B and C), $A_j$ is the open-data attraction heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:destination_attraction}), and $f_\theta$ is the Tanner decay function (Section~\ref{sec:decay_function}). The predicted probability of a trip falling into distance bin $b$ is the sum of predicted flows in that bin normalized by total predicted trips:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:predicted_prob_mle}
P_\theta(b) = \frac{\sum_{i,j : d_{ij} \in b} \hat{T}_{ij}(\theta)}{\sum_{i,j} \hat{T}_{ij}(\theta)}
\end{equation}

This predicted probability $P_\theta(b)$ explicitly incorporates zone geometry, spatial opportunity distribution ($A_j$), predicted trip generation ($O_i$), and the constraint structure, directly correcting for distance exposure. Note that the adaptive anchor parameter $\delta$ is fixed statically based on zone geometries (Equation~\ref{eq:delta}) prior to calibration; thus, the MLE optimization search space is restricted exclusively to the decay parameters $\theta = (\gamma, \beta)$. Assuming a multinomial distribution of trips across the distance bins, we estimate the decay parameters $\theta = (\gamma, \beta)$ by maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood (or equivalently, minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence) between the observed histogram fractions $\bar{y}_b = y_b / \sum_{b'} y_{b'}$ and the predicted probabilities $P_\theta(b)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:likelihood_mle}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{b=1}^{K} \bar{y}_b \log P_\theta(b)
\end{equation}

Minimizing $-\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ recovers the parameters $\theta$ from the target-city aggregate TLD without requiring individual zone-to-zone OD flow records, suggesting that aggregate, non-OD-revealing histograms can be empirically sufficient for high-fidelity decay calibration in practice within the scope of our evaluated models. Note, however, that in the fully deployed survey-free setting (Experiments B--C), $O_i$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:predicted_flow_mle}) is replaced by the GBDT-estimated $\hat{O}_i$, which carries its own prediction uncertainty. This joint estimation means that the MLE objective for decay parameters inherits production estimation errors, and the performance gap between oracle-outflow and deployed-outflow settings (CPC 0.7403 vs. 0.6860) reflects this coupled uncertainty, not solely decay recovery quality. The decomposed gravity formulation used in this study (referred to as the separable decomposed formulation for zero-shot transferable flows) is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_pipeline}.

\subsection{Distance Decay Function}\label{sec:decay_function}
We use the Tanner multiplicative deterrence function \citep{tanner1961}, which combines power-law and exponential terms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tanner_multi}
f(d_{ij};\,\beta,\gamma,\delta) = (d_{ij} + \delta)^{-\gamma} \cdot \exp(-\beta\,d_{ij})
\end{equation}
where $\beta > 0$ is the exponential decay rate governing long-range trip attenuation, $\gamma \ge 0$ is the power-law exponent modeling short-to-medium range trip distribution, and $\delta > 0$ is the adaptive geometric anchor. 

The offset $\delta$ prevents singularity at $d_{ij} = 0$ and adapts to zone size:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta}
\delta = 0.5 \times \bar{R}_{\mathrm{zone}}, \qquad \bar{R}_{\mathrm{zone}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Area}_i}{\pi}}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{R}_{\mathrm{zone}}$ is the mean zone radius. Parameters $(\beta, \gamma)$ are estimated via MLE on the aggregate distance-bin distribution. Calibration and optimization details are in the Appendix. Because only binned trip-length histograms are used—not individual OD records—this procedure is compatible with strict data-governance regulations (e.g., GDPR). For numerical implementation and computational efficiency, the theoretical offset $\delta$ is regularized by clamping the minimum distance to a small epsilon ($d_{\mathrm{min}} = 10^{-6}$), which yields mathematically equivalent results as $d_{ij} \gg d_{\mathrm{min}}$ for all cross-zone flows ($i \ne j$).

\subsection{Origin Outflow Component (\texorpdfstring{$O_i$}{O\_i})}\label{sec:origin_outflow}
The trip production $O_i = \sum_j T_{ij}$ is the total trips originating from zone $i$. We predict $O_i$ with a GBDT regressor trained on a parsimonious set of \textbf{6 core macroscopic features}: total population ($P_i$), total POI count ($\mathrm{POI}_i$), zone area ($\mathrm{Area}_i$), population density ($\rho_{pop, i}$), POI density ($\rho_{poi, i}$), and road density ($\mathrm{rd}_i$). This parsimonious feature set is chosen based on dimensional scaling in urban physics and Occam's razor (see Section~\ref{sec:transferable_features}), and avoids overfitting to source-domain geometries. Hyperparameters are in Appendix~C.

\subsection{Destination Attraction Component (\texorpdfstring{$A_j$}{A\_j})}\label{sec:destination_attraction}
The destination attraction component, $A_j$, represents the capacity of zone $j$ to attract trips, acting as a proxy for local spatial opportunities (e.g., employment, commerce, and residential destinations) \citep{wilson1971, Simini2012universal}. In traditional gravity models, $A_j$ is typically estimated using supervised regression models or calibrated zone-specific parameters. However, in zero-shot cross-city transfer scenarios where target-city commuting flows are completely unavailable, supervised estimation of destination attraction poses a significant risk of overfitting to the source domain.

To address this challenge and ensure robust zero-shot generalizability, we formulate a training-free, scale-invariant opportunity heuristic $\hat{A}_j$. This heuristic combines two distinct dimensions of spatial opportunities: residential capacity (reflecting return-to-home flows) and activity-based opportunities (reflecting work, shopping, and leisure destinations). Formally, we define:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:attraction_heuristic}
\hat{A}_j = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \mathrm{minmax}(P_j) + \mathrm{minmax}(\mathrm{POI}_j) \right]
\end{equation}
where $P_j$ represents the gridded population count of zone $j$ (sourced from the same third-party dataset used for target OD flows during evaluation to maintain alignment, as described in Section~\ref{sec:data_sources}, though deployable with open products like WorldPop), and $\mathrm{POI}_j$ represents the total count of commercial, industrial, and social Points of Interest (POIs) extracted from OpenStreetMap. 

Because population and POI counts operate on vastly different numerical scales across heterogeneous metropolitan areas, we apply a min-max scaling function to project both features into a standardized interval $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minmax}
\mathrm{minmax}(x_j) = \frac{x_j - \min_{k \in \mathcal{V}} x_k}{\max_{k \in \mathcal{V}} x_k - \min_{k \in \mathcal{V}} x_k + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{V}$ denotes the set of all zones within the target metropolitan area, and $\epsilon = 10^{-9}$ is a small positive constant to prevent division by zero. This scaling preserves the relative hierarchy of attractiveness within a city while normalizing absolute scale differences between different metropolitan areas. In Section~\ref{sec:shapley_game}, the ablation analysis (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) validates that this training-free heuristic contributes meaningfully to zero-shot performance, with its removal causing a 4.4\% CPC drop, suggesting that the simplified formulation is empirically sufficient within the evaluated scope without introducing source-domain overfitting.

\subsection{Data Firewall and Zero-Leakage Protocol}\label{sec:data_firewall}
To guarantee that target-city commuting matrices are never leaked into the model components, we implement a strict data firewall between source-city and target-city variables across all phases of the transfer learning pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Outflow Predictor Firewall}: The GBDT outflow predictor is trained exclusively on built-environment features and ground-truth trip counts from source cities. Target-city spatial features are only used during inference, ensuring that the outflow predictions contain no target OD flow information.
  \item \textbf{Decay Parameter Firewall}: City-specific distance-decay parameters ($\hat{\gamma}, \hat{\beta}$) are recovered solely from the target city's aggregate 1D trip-length distribution (Setting 2). No source-city parameters or target-city zone-to-zone flows are mixed into this process.
  \item \textbf{Attraction Heuristic Firewall}: The attraction heuristic uses only the target city's population and POI feature hierarchies via a training-free min-max function, transferring no parameters from source cities.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation Firewall}: Target-city zone-to-zone OD matrices are kept completely blind to all model components, used solely as the test ground-truth to calculate CPC and RMSE.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design Principle: Open-Data-Compatible}\label{sec:open_data_compatible}
All predictive features in the proposed approach are drawn from globally available open data rather than proprietary cellular records or household surveys, making the methodology deployable in any city. We intentionally exclude proprietary localized employment registries, which lack global equivalents, and instead proxy employment activity through commercial and industrial POI densities from OpenStreetMap. Population counts in this study are sourced from the third-party mobility dataset. In future deployments, these may be replaced by globally available gridded products such as WorldPop \citep{worldpop2018}, but this substitution is not evaluated in the present study.

Ground-truth origin-destination (OD) flows between census tracts are obtained from a third-party human mobility dataset covering 50 US metropolitan areas. For the distance-decay prior, we evaluate two aggregate sources: (1) a \textbf{third-party aggregate baseline}, in which the same third-party OD dataset is aggregated into distance-bin histograms---this serves as an oracle bound, not a claim of data independence; and (2) the \textbf{Meta Movement Distribution Maps} \citep{MetaMovementDistributionMaps}, independently collected county-level aggregate statistics that do not derive from the same ground-truth OD source---this is the true OD-free, independently sourced calibration scenario. In both cases, the aggregate prior does not provide zone-to-zone OD matrices. The OD matrix serves as the ground-truth target for city-specific evaluation and as training labels for source cities' production models; it is never used as a predictive feature for target prediction. By showing that independently sourced aggregate decay curves (Meta MDM) yield downstream performance comparable to full OD matrices (Section~\ref{sec:results}), we provide empirical evidence that publicly released aggregate mobility products may serve as a viable alternative for calibrating spatial interaction models, avoiding the need for proprietary or privacy-invasive individual surveys in practical planning pipelines.

\subsubsection{OSM-Derived Features}
We extract POIs and road features using the Overpass API from OpenStreetMap snapshots \citep{atwal2025commuting}. POI counts are calculated across 16 land-use categories (e.g., retail, industrial, education, health).

\subsubsection{Population Data}
Population counts are sourced from the third-party mobility dataset used for ground-truth OD flows. In future deployments, these may be replaced by globally available gridded products such as WorldPop \citep{worldpop2018}, but this substitution is not evaluated in the present study.

\subsection{Meta MDM Harmonization}\label{sec:meta_mdm_harmonization}
To calibrate the distance-decay function using independently sourced tech-platform telemetry, we harmonize the county-level Meta Movement Distribution Maps (MDM) with the census-tract-level metropolitan boundaries used in our evaluation:
\begin{description}
  \item[Spatial Join \& Boundary Mismatch] Meta MDM provides aggregate travel fractions at the county level. To reconstruct the aggregate trip-length distribution (TLD) for a target metropolitan area (which is defined by a collection of census tracts), we identify all counties intersecting the metropolitan footprint. When a metropolitan area overlaps multiple counties or when county boundaries do not align with city limits, we compute the population share of each intersecting county using WorldPop gridded data. The county-level TLDs are then combined into a single composite metropolitan TLD using a weighted average based on these population shares:
  \begin{equation}
  p_{\text{metro}}(b) = \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} w_c \cdot p_c(b)
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of intersecting counties, $p_c(b)$ is the travel fraction for bin $b$ in county $c$, and $w_c = P_c^{\text{metro}} / \sum_{c'} P_{c'}^{\text{metro}}$ is the normalized population weight of county $c$ within the metropolitan area.
  \item[Bin Mapping \& Thresholds] Meta MDM groups trips into 3 physical distance bins designed to protect individual privacy: Bin 1: $[0, 10\text{ km}]$, Bin 2: $(10, 100\text{ km}]$, and Bin 3: $>100\text{ km}$. To map the continuous centroid-to-centroid distances $d_{ij}$ calculated between census tracts to these categories, we assign each tract pair $(i, j)$ to one of the 3 bins based on its geometric distance.
  \item[Normalization] The predicted probability of a trip falling into Meta bin $b \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ is computed by summing the predicted gravity flows $T_{ij}(\theta)$ within the corresponding distance range, and normalizing by the total predicted flow:
  \begin{equation}
  P_\theta(b) = \frac{\sum_{i,j : d_{ij} \in \text{Range}(b)} \hat{T}_{ij}(\theta)}{\sum_{i,j} \hat{T}_{ij}(\theta)}
  \end{equation}
  This normalization bridges the continuous theoretical gravity formulation with the discrete, privacy-masked categories of Meta MDM, allowing MLE parameter estimation (Equation~\ref{eq:likelihood_mle}) to proceed without disaggregate spatial data.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Candidate Feature Set (30 dimensions)}
We initially construct 30 candidate features per zone. This candidate space comprises 23 scale and density variables (zone area, road length, residential population, total POI counts, 16 POI categories, and 3 density metrics: population density, POI density, and road density) and 7 city-wide Z-score context features. These 7 context features are constructed by applying Z-score normalization to the 7 key population and infrastructural density features that exhibit high variance across cities. The Z-score context features are defined as:
\begin{equation}
z_{ip} = \frac{x_{ip} - \mu_p^{(c)}}{\sigma_p^{(c)}}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_p^{(c)}$ and $\sigma_p^{(c)}$ represent the mean and standard deviation of feature $p$ across all zones in city $c$. These context features normalize absolute scale differences, capturing the relative hierarchy of zones within their respective metropolitan areas.

\subsubsection{SHAP-Based Feature Stability Selection}
To identify features that generalize across cities, we apply SHAP importance analysis \citep{lundberg2017unified} to the GBDT production model. Features are ranked by a cross-city stability score $S_p = \mu_p\,/\,(\sigma_p + \epsilon)$, where $\mu_p$ and $\sigma_p$ denote the mean and standard deviation of per-city GBDT SHAP importances and $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$. A feature is retained if $\mu_p \ge 0.005$ and $S_p \ge 1.0$, ensuring both relevance and cross-city consistency. This analysis identifies 23 stable features from the 30-candidate space (23.3\% dimensionality reduction) and confirms that the 6 core structural features used in the deployed model (Section~\ref{sec:origin_outflow}) are among the primary and most stable predictors, with no accuracy loss relative to the full candidate set (Appendix~C). \textit{Note: this feature-level analysis is distinct from the inter-component Shapley game in Section~\ref{sec:shapley_game}, which quantifies the relative contribution of the three model components $f(d)$, $O_i$, and $A_j$.}

\subsection{Urban Morphology Grouping (RQ3)}\label{sec:urban_morphology}
To investigate RQ3---under what morphological conditions aggregate-calibrated decay is most effective---the 25 held-out target cities are classified into four morphological groups based on physical geography and urban structure. The grouping follows established urban morphology criteria \citep{gonzalez2008understanding,brockmann2006scaling}:
\begin{description}
  \item[Dense / Transit (5 cities):] High-density urban cores with extensive rapid transit networks (e.g., subway, light rail), where multi-modal options decouple travel distance from actual trip cost. Distance friction is weakest in these environments. \textit{Cities: Boston, Long Beach, New York, Philadelphia, Washington DC.} (Note: Long Beach is grouped here due to its high density, though its standalone transit network is less extensive than New York or Boston).
  \item[Sprawling / Grid (10 cities):] Low-to-medium density cities built on regular road grids, with car-dependent mobility patterns. Distance decay is moderate and relatively uniform. \textit{Cities: Atlanta, Dallas, El Paso, Houston, Jacksonville, Las Vegas, Mesa, Omaha, San Antonio, Tulsa.}
  \item[Polycentric (7 cities):] Cities with multiple distinct employment and activity centers, where flows cluster around nodes rather than following simple distance gradients. \textit{Cities: Albuquerque, Charlotte, Detroit, Louisville, Milwaukee, Raleigh, San Jose.}
  \item[Coastal (3 cities):] Cities where coastlines or topographic barriers physically constrain the road network, creating strong geographic separation between zones. The constrained geography is hypothesized to align with a stronger influence of physical distance friction, making structured models highly effective. \textit{Cities: Portland, Seattle, Virginia Beach.}
\end{description}
This grouping enables direct investigation of how physical geography modulates the role of distance decay as a transferable component, providing empirical scope for the aggregate calibration approach.

\subsection{Cross-City Transfer Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:transfer_protocol}
Standard mobility evaluations split OD pairs within a single city, which leaks spatial information from test zones into training. We instead use a strict \textbf{25/25 Stratified Split}: The 50 US metropolitan areas are divided into two disjoint sets of 25 training source cities and 25 held-out target cities. Models are trained exclusively on pooled data from the source cities and evaluated on the held-out target cities without any access to target-city OD data or retraining. The split is stratified by geographic region and urban morphology to ensure statistical representation across city types.

\subsubsection{Information Leakage Prevention}
Under this cross-city evaluation protocol, we enforce a strict information firewall between the source and target environments. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Forbidden}: Disaggregate or OD-revealing target-city data (e.g., zone-to-zone flow matrices $T_{ij}$, household travel survey records, raw cellular trajectories, and disaggregate trip-demand logs).
  \item \textbf{Allowed}: Aggregate or non-OD-revealing target data (specifically target-city aggregate trip-length distributions for calibration), centroid coordinates, administrative zone boundaries, OpenStreetMap POIs, and gridded open demography (WorldPop).
\end{itemize}
Since destination attraction is computed via a training-free open-data heuristic and distance decay parameters are recovered from target-city aggregate trip-length distributions (or fall back to source-trained national priors), zero-shot leakage of target-city OD flows is mathematically prevented.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metric: Common Part of Commuters}
We measure prediction quality with the Common Part of Commuters (CPC) \citep{lenormand2012universal}:
\begin{equation}
\text{CPC}(T, \hat{T}) = \frac{2 \sum_{i,j} \min(T_{ij}, \hat{T}_{ij})}{\sum_{i,j} T_{ij} + \sum_{i,j} \hat{T}_{ij}}
\end{equation}
where $T_{ij}$ represents the ground-truth commuter flow from zone $i$ to zone $j$, and $\hat{T}_{ij}$ is the predicted flow. The CPC index ranges from 0 (indicating completely disjoint flow matrices) to 1 (indicating perfect prediction).

\subsection{Experiment A: Decay Parameter Recovery (Answering RQ1)}\label{sec:experiment_a}
We begin by evaluating whether trip-length distributions alone contain sufficient information to recover the distance-decay structure (Experiment A, addressing RQ1). This question is foundational: if the decay structure is recoverable from aggregate data, and if distance decay is theoretically modeled as a primary transferable component in spatial interaction models, then aggregate calibration becomes a viable path to zero-shot prediction.

\subsection{Component Contributions via Shapley Game (Answering RQ2)}\label{sec:shapley_game}
To evaluate the relative structural importance of each gravity component for zero-shot transfer (RQ2), we conduct a factorial ablation across the 25 held-out cities. Each component is disabled in turn---replaced by a uniform-weight fallback---to isolate its marginal contribution. Crucially, the following analysis evaluates the proposed decomposition hypothesis within the mathematical constraints of our gravity framework, rather than asserting a universal, model-independent physical law of human mobility. The resulting contributions reflect how predictive capacity is distributed among the structural components under the assumed model equations, and should be interpreted as diagnostic bounds of this specific architecture.

To resolve the mathematical asymmetry of one-at-a-time ablation and evaluate the intrinsic marginal value of each component fairly, we implement a game-theoretic analysis using Shapley values \citep{shapley1953value} across all $2^3 = 8$ possible component coalitions. Over the uniform flow baseline (CPC 0.4263), the Shapley values attribute a mean CPC gain of 0.2258 (85.8\% of total gain) to distance decay $f(d)$, 0.0208 (7.9\% of total gain) to attractiveness $A_j$, and 0.0167 (6.4\% of total gain) to outflow production $O_i$. 

This indicates that distance decay is the structurally dominant component in predictive performance. Crucially, this high structural importance of decay explains why recovery of the decay parameters (investigated in RQ1) is a primary determinant of spatial pattern recovery. However, this component importance analysis holds decay fixed at the national prior, and should not be conflated with the marginal benefit of city-specific calibration itself: as shown in Table~\ref{tab:multibaseline_comparison}, switching from the national prior to city-specific Meta MDM calibration does not improve the mean CPC (0.6896 vs 0.6860), which suggests that while having a decay term is critical, a coarse national prior can perform comparably to a highly privacy-masked 3-bin localized prior due to the information loss inherent in physical binning constraints. 

Furthermore, this connection highlights the practical importance of the aggregate-based recovery validated in RQ1. Because the distance-decay parameters recovered from aggregate trip-length distributions are nearly identical to the oracle parameters fitted from complete OD matrices, the primary predictive contribution attributed to the distance-decay component in RQ2 can be interpreted as fully transferable in practice using only aggregate representations that minimize privacy exposure compared with individual trajectories. This effectively bridges the theoretical dominance of the decay component with a viable, privacy-compliant deployment mechanism.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance Testing}
To assess the statistical reliability of our comparisons, we report median performance, bootstrap confidence intervals, and multiple-comparison corrected significance tests.
For the 25 held-out target cities, we obtain the following bootstrapped 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) for the mean CPC (10,000 resamples): Proposed Model (Survey-Free) mean 0.6860 (95\% CI: [0.6706, 0.7002], median 0.6907, IQR 0.0450); Proposed Model (Oracle $O_i$) mean 0.7337 (95\% CI: [0.7233, 0.7430], median 0.7341, IQR 0.0273); DeepGravity (Zero-Shot) mean 0.7529 (95\% CI: [0.7409, 0.7628], median 0.7517, IQR 0.0295); and locally-calibrated Traditional Tanner Gravity mean 0.7382 (95\% CI: [0.7250, 0.7496], median 0.7391, IQR 0.0367).

Applying the Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\citep{wilcoxon1945} across the target cities, we correct for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni adjustment (three pairwise tests). Under oracle outflow conditions ($O_i$), a statistically significant difference is detected between the proposed model (Oracle $O_i$) and locally-calibrated Traditional Gravity (Wilcoxon raw $p = 0.00737$, Bonferroni-adjusted $p = 0.02211$). However, the absolute performance gap is extremely small ($\Delta\text{CPC} = -0.0045$, Cohen's $d = -0.15$), indicating that the difference is practically negligible. To formally evaluate whether the two models are practically equivalent, we perform a Two One-Sided Test (TOST) equivalence test with a pre-specified equivalence margin of $\Delta_{\text{eq}} = 0.01$ CPC, corresponding to a one-percentage-point tolerance in the CPC similarity index. This margin represents approximately 30\% of the standard deviation of target-city performance, which is well within the typical 10\% to 50\% standard deviation threshold commonly used in statistical equivalence testing. The TOST rejects the null hypothesis of non-equivalence ($p = 0.00326$, well below the $\alpha=0.05$ significance level), formally establishing that the aggregate-calibrated gravity model is equivalent to the locally-fit gravity model within the pre-specified margin. Note that this statistical equivalence is established specifically for the oracle outflow control variant; the fully deployed survey-free model (CPC 0.6860) is not statistically equivalent to the locally-calibrated Tanner model (0.7382), as the production prediction errors degrade the zero-shot performance when oracle outflows are unavailable.

The proposed model strongly and significantly outperforms the parameter-free Radiation Model baseline ($\Delta\text{CPC} = +0.2512$, Wilcoxon Bonferroni-adjusted $p = 1.78 \times 10^{-7}$). The gap versus DeepGravity (Oracle $O_i$) is statistically significant (Wilcoxon raw $p = 1.2 \times 10^{-6}$, Bonferroni-adjusted $p = 3.6 \times 10^{-6}$), reflecting the expected advantage of a high-capacity neural model over a parsimonious physical structure.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The three research questions (RQ1--RQ3) defined in Section~1 correspond directly to the three gaps (G1--G3) identified in Table~\ref{tab:literature_comparison}: G1 addressed by demonstrating aggregate-based decay recovery (Section~\ref{sec:experiment_a}), G2 by quantifying decay dominance via Shapley analysis (Section~\ref{sec:shapley_game}), and G3 by the exploratory morphological evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:experiment_c}). The following discussion distinguishes rigorously established empirical findings from claims that warrant further cross-regional verification.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence (Established Findings)}
Our empirical results across 50 US metropolitan areas provide direct statistical evidence for the following three claims within the evaluated scope:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{High-Fidelity Decay Parameter Recovery}: We empirically demonstrate that aggregate trip-length histograms preserve sufficient information to estimate per-city distance decay curves. The recovered exponents correlate strongly ($r = 0.909$ for the power-law exponent $\gamma$) with those fitted on individual-level OD flows.
  \item \textbf{TOST Equivalence under Oracle Outflows}: When target production totals are controlled (Setting 3), we formally establish via a Two One-Sided Test (TOST) that the aggregate-calibrated gravity model is equivalent to the locally-calibrated Tanner gravity model within a tight margin of $\Delta_{\text{eq}} = 0.01$ CPC (corresponding to a one-percentage-point tolerance in the CPC similarity index, $p = 0.00326$).
  \item \textbf{Decay Dominance within the Decomposed Gravity Game}: Using game-theoretic Shapley values, the results support the finding that distance decay is the dominant contributor to zero-shot prediction capacity (85.8\% of CPC gain) specifically within our separable gravity framework, providing a mathematical justification for why local decay calibration is the critical step for zero-shot transfer.
\end{enumerate}
The empirical recovery performance supports---but does not mathematically prove---the separability approximation adopted in this study.

\subsection{Conceptual Intuition: Marginal Projection under Separability}\label{sec:conceptual_intuition}
To understand why aggregate trip-length distributions contain sufficient information to recover city-specific distance-decay parameters, it is helpful to examine the problem from a probabilistic perspective. The full origin-destination (OD) matrix represents a discrete joint probability distribution $P(X, Y)$ of trips over a two-dimensional spatial grid of origins $X$ and destinations $Y$. The trip-length distribution (TLD) represents the projection of this joint distribution onto a one-dimensional distance domain $D$. Formally, the TLD is the marginal distribution of travel distances:
\begin{equation}
P(D = d) = \sum_{x \in X} \sum_{y \in Y} P(X=x, Y=y) \cdot \mathbf{1}[d_{xy} \approx d]
\end{equation}

Under the proposed decomposed gravity hypothesis, the joint probability is structurally constrained. The flow between any zone pair is separable into independent factors representing origin production capacity, destination attraction capacity, and distance deterrence. Because the distance decay function $f(d)$ is the only component in the joint model that depends explicitly on the spatial distance $d_{xy}$, the projection of $P(X,Y)$ onto the distance dimension $D$ preserves the shape of $f(d)$. 

While the spatial distribution of opportunities (the arrangement of population masses $O_i$ and POI centers $A_j$) acts as a geometric filter that distorts the raw marginal distribution $P(D)$, this distortion is purely a function of the known built environment. The exposure-corrected MLE (Equation~\ref{eq:likelihood_mle}) acts as a deconvolution mechanism: it utilizes the target city's known spatial configurations to filter out the geometric exposure bias, thereby isolating the latent distance-decay parameters. In other words, because the decay behavior is assumed to be a uniform, city-wide spatial friction, it manifests consistently across all zone pairs, allowing its parametric shape to be reconstructed from the marginal distance distribution once local exposure is controlled.


\subsection{Hypotheses Requiring Further Verification}
While our empirical evidence is robust, several key claims remain hypotheses that require further cross-regional verification:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Global Transferability}: Whether the learned GBDT production weights and the open-data attraction heuristic generalize outside the United States (e.g., to dense Asian cities or informal settlements in developing countries) remains a hypothesis.
  \item \textbf{Physical Causality}: We show that distance decay functions as the central explanatory driver of predictive performance. However, this statistical association does not prove that geometric distance causally determines travel behavior. In reality, distance is a proxy for travel time, transport cost, and individual behavioral constraints.
  \item \textbf{Separability Approximation}: The assumption that spatial interaction components (production, attraction, decay) are strictly separable and independent remains a structural approximation. In highly integrated transit networks, these components likely exhibit non-linear coupling.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Deployment Implications}
By demonstrating that high-fidelity decay calibration is achievable using only target-city aggregate histograms, this study enables a new paradigm for deploying mobility models. Historically, calibrating per-city gravity models required target household travel surveys or invasive individual-level GPS telemetry, both of which raise significant privacy concerns and are restricted under frameworks like GDPR. 

By leveraging globally available open-data features (OSM, WorldPop) and target-city aggregate representations (like the Meta Movement Distribution Maps), planning agencies can calibrate and deploy structured, interpretable gravity models in data-scarce or highly regulated environments. This bypasses the need for proprietary survey collection while maintaining strict data firewalls.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats_to_validity}
To ensure the objective interpretation of our findings, we systematically categorize the threats to the validity of our transfer learning workflow:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[Target Mobility Dependency (Not Pure Zero-Shot)] The primary proposed calibration setting (Setting 2) relies on target-city aggregate TLDs to calibrate parameters. While this bypasses individual zone-to-zone OD surveys, it still requires aggregate target-city travel data. Although we evaluate a pure zero-shot fallback (Setting 1) requiring zero target mobility data, under deployed GBDT outflows, Setting 1 actually outperforms Setting 2 slightly (CPC 0.6896 vs 0.6860), suggesting that localized calibration from highly binned target mobility telemetry does not necessarily yield downstream gains compared to a robust national fallback. Under oracle outflows, the subset target CPC is 0.6839 for Setting 1 versus 0.7080 under the Meta MDM prior (Setting 2).
  
  \item[Data Source Independence and Overestimation Risk] In Experiment A, the aggregate histograms used for the oracle comparison are synthetically compiled from the target ground-truth OD matrix. While useful as a diagnostic bound, this does not capture data alignment mismatches of real-world deployment. When switching to independently sourced Meta MDM data, the oracle outflow CPC drops from $0.7403$ (synthetic 20-bin oracle) to $0.7080$ (Meta MDM 3-bin), a gap of $-4.36\%$, suggesting that synthetic aggregation overestimates real-world calibration quality due to different bin configurations and data sources.
  
  \item \textbf{Morphology Sample Constraints (Small-n)}: The morphological classification of target cities is manually defined, and some categories have small sample sizes (Coastal, $n=3$). While the performance differences align with urban geography theories, they are exploratory and lack the statistical power required for generalized claims.
  
  \item \textbf{Spatial Error Blindness}: The Common Part of Commuters (CPC) is a global similarity index. It evaluates overall matrix overlap but is blind to localized spatial error structures, such as systematic prediction errors along specific high-volume transit corridors.
  
  \item \textbf{Euclidean Distance Simplification}: We proxy travel distance using Euclidean distance between zone centroids. This ignores network routing circuity, travel time, and multi-modal transit friction, which decouple travel from geometric distance in dense transit environments.
  
  \item \textbf{Geographic Generalizability}: The empirical evaluation is restricted to US cities. Commuting patterns, urban density profiles, and land-use arrangements in these cities may not represent those in Europe, Asia, or South America.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Conclusions}

This study investigated the zero-shot cross-city transfer problem through a three-tier conceptual architecture, moving away from ad-hoc calibration toward a structural understanding of urban spatial interaction. By focusing on 50 US metropolitan areas, we established that: (1) the city-specific distance-decay function represents the primary transferable object of spatial flow, (2) the aggregate trip-length distribution acts as a privacy-preserving observable projection that yields sufficient information to identify this object, and (3) this relationship can be successfully leveraged for zero-shot gravity model calibration. Our work leads to three core conclusions:

\subsection{What Did We Discover?}
Empirically, we demonstrated that the target-city aggregate trip-length distribution (TLD)—even when heavily compressed into a small number of physical distance bins (e.g., Meta Movement Distribution Maps)—contains sufficient signal to identify the parameters of the distance-decay function. Under controlled oracle outflows, calibration on this aggregate measurement yields downstream prediction performance statistically equivalent to models calibrated directly on target-city individual-level OD flows. Mechanistically, our game-theoretic Shapley value analysis showed that distance decay dominates predictive capacity within this decomposed gravity framework, confirming its role as the primary transferable object. However, switch-testing also revealed that localized calibration from real-world telemetry does not significantly outperform a robust national decay fallback, indicating that the practical utility of localized calibration is heavily bounded by the resolution and binning constraints of the observable measurement.

\subsection{Why Does It Matter?}
These findings matter because they redefine how spatial interaction models can be calibrated in data-scarce and privacy-regulated environments. Historically, identifying city-specific travel friction required highly sensitive individual-level surveys or proprietary cellular trajectories. By demonstrating that the primary transferable object (distance decay) can be robustly identified using a one-dimensional observable projection (the TLD), we provide urban planners with a deployable, survey-free forecasting pipeline. This architecture enables the calibration of structured, interpretable gravity models using only globally available open spatial data (OSM, WorldPop) and privacy-compliant aggregate indicators, bypassing GDPR constraints and reducing survey costs.

\subsection{What Remains Unknown?}
Despite these contributions, several structural elements of the urban mobility process remain unresolved:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Limits of the Separability Approximation}: While component decomposition (separating production, attraction, and decay) enables transferability, these components are likely non-linearly coupled in reality. How to model this coupled interaction while preserving zero-shot transferability remains unknown.
  \item \textbf{Generalizability Beyond US Geographies}: The empirical evidence is restricted to US metropolitan areas characterized by high private vehicle usage and specific density gradients. The transferability of these models to highly dense, multi-modal transit networks (e.g., in European or East Asian megacities) or informal settlements in the Global South requires further cross-regional verification.
  \item \textbf{Network Routing and Time-Distance Friction}: Centroid-to-centroid Euclidean distance was used as a proxy for travel friction. Incorporating network routing, congestion-induced travel times, and multi-modal transit friction into the aggregate calibration process remains an open direction for future work.
\end{itemize}

Parameters $(\gamma, \beta)$ are estimated via MLE from the aggregate trip-length histogram using L-BFGS-B optimization in log-space with 10 random restarts to avoid local optima. Full implementation details are available in the code repository.

\subsubsection*{A.2 Robustness Analysis}
We evaluate the robustness of our MLE parameter recovery under non-ideal data scenarios:
\begin{description}
  \item[Bin Sensitivity Analysis] Sweeping the distance bins $K \in \{3, 5, 10, 20\}$ shows that downstream prediction performance stabilizes at a high level. Specifically, the mean CPC across target cities is $0.7346$ for $K=3$, $0.7367$ for $K=5$, $0.7391$ for $K=10$, and $0.7398$ for $K=20$ (compared to the full OD ground truth baseline of $0.7411$). This indicates that even extremely coarse spatial data (e.g., 5 to 10 equal-width bins) is empirically sufficient within the evaluated scope to robustly capture the decay profile and predict commuting flows.
  \item[Missing Attractiveness Data ($A_j$)] Randomly removing 10\%, 20\%, and 50\% of target-city destination attractions $A_j$ yields highly stable decay recovery (e.g., $\gamma_{\text{MAE}} = 0.0395$ under 20\% missing data vs. $0.0347$ baseline). This validates the robustness of aggregate MLE against localized data gaps.
  \item[CBD Collapse (Extreme $A_j$ Distortion)] Disrupting the attraction $A_j$ of the top 10\% highest attractiveness zones (CBD collapse) still allows accurate parameter recovery under two scenarios: replacing affected zones with the city-wide mean attraction ($\gamma_{\text{mae}} = 0.0441$) and scaling them down by 80\% ($\gamma_{\text{mae}} = 0.0486$). This suggests that spatial decay friction can be recovered even when key destination hubs are heavily distorted.
\end{description}

\subsection*{Appendix B. Robustness of Origin Production Transfer}

We analyze the sensitivity of zero-shot production transfer across two dimensions: training-set scale and prediction noise.

\subsubsection*{B.1 Source-Scale Sensitivity}
We analyze how the number of training environments affects zero-shot predictability by retraining the GBDT on subsets of $n_{\text{src}} \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 25\}$ source cities. Target-city evaluation shows that zero-shot prediction performance rises steeply from $n_{\text{src}} = 5$ and saturates around 15–20 source cities. This indicates that only a modest number of source cities is required to learn stable, generalizable outflow characteristics.

\subsubsection*{B.2 Noise Sensitivity}
To evaluate the stability of origin transfer under prediction errors, we inject additive Gaussian noise at log-scale ($\sigma \in \{10\%, 20\%, 40\%\}$) directly onto the predicted outflows $\log\hat{O}_i$. Under substantial injected noise (40\%), the target CPC degrades from $0.6896$ to $0.6626$. While this $-3.9\%$ drop is larger than the isolated production component contribution (3.6\% Shapley value), it demonstrates that even highly corrupted outflow inputs do not collapse the spatial interaction matrix, because the production-constraint normalization restricts the error propagation.
